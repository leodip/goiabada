This page helps you choose the rule every new password must meet.

**Password policy** under **Admin**, **General** applies whenever a password is set: on the registration, activation and reset forms, under **Account**, **Change password**, when an administrator creates a user or gives one a new password, through the Admin and Account APIs, and in the `recover` command. Every page that takes a new password shows the rule under the field before anything is refused, except under No policy, which has nothing to show, and a refused password is told which rule it broke.

## Choose a policy

<Steps>

1. In the admin console, open **Admin**, **General**.

2. Under **Password policy**, choose a card. **NIST SP 800-63B** comes first, and it's the one we recommend.

3. Click **Save**. The next password each user sets is held to it. Passwords already set aren't checked again: see [what switching does](#what-switching-does).

</Steps>

## The policies

| Policy | API value | At least | Also |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NIST SP 800-63B** | `nist` | 15 characters | Any characters at all, with no kind required. A commonly used password is refused, and so is one too close to the account's details or the app's name. |
| **No policy** | `none` | 1 character | Nothing |
| **Low** | `low` | 6 characters | Nothing |
| **Medium** | `medium` | 8 characters | A lowercase letter, an uppercase letter and a number |
| **High** | `high` | 10 characters | A lowercase letter, an uppercase letter, a number and a symbol |

Every policy accepts at most 72 bytes, the most bcrypt hashes. A character outside ASCII takes two to four of them. Each Unicode code point counts as one character toward the minimum.

The four older policies fall short of NIST SP 800-63B, and their cards say so. They stay for an installation that answers to another rule, such as PCI DSS 4.0 requirement 8.3.6, which asks for 12 characters with letters and digits. Choosing one is a departure from the guideline you make on purpose.

The general settings API sends and takes the policy as `passwordPolicy`, with the values above. See [`PUT /api/v1/admin/settings/general`](/reference/api/admin/operations/updatesettingsgeneral/).

## What a new installation starts with

| Setting | Default |
|---|---|
| **Password policy** | NIST SP 800-63B (`nist` in the API) |

An upgraded installation keeps the policy it had, so one on Low stays on Low until you switch. Nothing is migrated.

The first administrator's password, which the first start seeds and the [setup wizard](/deploy/setup-wizard/) writes, always meets the NIST rule, whatever the policy: at least 15 characters, not a commonly used password, and not too close to `GOIABADA_ADMIN_EMAIL` or `GOIABADA_APPNAME`. See [`GOIABADA_ADMIN_PASSWORD`](/reference/environment-variables/).

## What NIST SP 800-63B asks, and why

NIST SP 800-63B is the US National Institute of Standards and Technology's guideline for how people sign in. The `nist` policy follows its fourth revision, SP 800-63B-4, section 3.1.1.2, for a password that may be the only thing a sign-in checks:

- **Length.** At least 15 characters, each Unicode code point counting as one. Length is what makes a password hard to guess.
- **No composition rules.** The verifier "SHALL NOT impose other composition rules", such as asking for an uppercase letter, a digit or a symbol. They make a password harder to remember without making it harder to guess: people answer them with `Password1!`.
- **A blocklist.** Every new password is compared against a list of commonly used, expected or compromised passwords. "The entire password SHALL be subject to comparison, not substrings or words that might be contained therein."
- **Guidance and a reason.** The verifier offers guidance on choosing a strong password, and gives the reason when it refuses one.
- **No forced changes.** Passwords aren't changed on a schedule, only on evidence that one was compromised.

An attacker guessing online gets 100 wrong passwords an hour for one account, so they spend them on the passwords people pick most. The minimum length and the blocklist together take those away. See [rate limits](/reference/environment-variables/#rate-limits).

Goiabada has no password hints and no security questions, which the guideline forbids too.

## Common passwords

Under `nist`, a password on a built-in list of commonly used passwords is refused. The list is the 16,043 passwords of 15 characters or more, and at most 72 bytes, in three public lists, lowercased and with duplicates removed:

| List | Licence |
|---|---|
| The UK National Cyber Security Centre's 100,000 most used passwords, drawn from Have I Been Pwned's Pwned Passwords | Crown copyright, under the Open Government Licence v3.0; Pwned Passwords under CC BY 4.0 |
| Pwdb's 1,000,000 most common passwords (2020) | MIT |
| The 1,000,000 most common of xato's ten million passwords (2015) | MIT |

All three come from [SecLists](https://github.com/danielmiessler/SecLists), under the MIT licence, at a pinned commit, each checked against its SHA-256 when the list is generated. The list is built into the auth server and the setup wizard, so checking a password makes no network call and works on an installation with no internet access. A release refreshes it.

The whole password is compared with each entry, ignoring case, so `passwordpassword` and `PasswordPassword` are refused, while a passphrase that merely contains a listed password isn't. Shorter common passwords aren't on the list, since the 15-character minimum already refuses them.

Sign-in never consults the list: it only checks the password against its hash.

## The account's details

Under `nist`, a password is also refused when it's the account's email address, the part before the `@`, the username or the app name, or one of those written twice or more, or padded with digits, symbols or spaces at either end. Case is ignored. For `jane.doe@example.com`, with the username `janedoe`, in an app named Goiabada, these are all refused:

- `jane.doe@example.com`
- `jane.doe2026!!!!!!`
- `JaneDoeJaneDoe2026`
- `goiabada2026!!!!!`
- `GoiabadaGoiabada`

A passphrase that merely contains one of them, such as `jane likes long walks`, isn't. A registration has no username yet, so it's compared with the email address and the app name alone.

## How a refusal reads

Length is checked first. A password the `nist` policy refuses reads one of these on the page, and the Admin and Account APIs answer `400` with its key as the `error_code`:

| `error_code` | Message |
|---|---|
| `validator.password.too_short` | Use at least 15 characters for the password. |
| `validator.password.too_long` | Use at most 72 bytes for the password. Accented and other non-English characters count as two or more bytes each. |
| `validator.password.too_common` | This password is one attackers try first. Choose another: a few unrelated words make a long password that's easy to remember. |
| `validator.password.too_close_to_account` | This password is too close to the account's email address, username or the app's name. Choose another. |

Under the field, the pages read "At least 15 characters. A few unrelated words make a long password that's easy to remember. Common passwords, and the account's email address or the app's name, are refused." See [the API's errors](/reference/api/errors/).

## Why no policy asks for 8 characters

NIST SP 800-63B-4 allows a minimum of 8 characters for a password that's only ever used beside a second factor. No policy here does, because no password here is known never to be the only factor: a client at `urn:goiabada:level1` signs in with the password alone, `urn:goiabada:level2_optional` skips the code for a user without two-factor authentication, and the [password grant](/legacy-flows/ropc/) checks nothing else. See [ACR and AMR](/concepts/acr-and-amr/).

## Where it falls short of NIST

The `nist` policy meets every SHALL of section 3.1.1.2. It departs from two of its SHOULDs:

- **The maximum is 72 bytes, not 64 characters.** The guideline says the verifier "SHOULD permit a maximum password length of at least 64 characters". bcrypt hashes at most 72 bytes, so that's the limit: 72 characters of ASCII, 36 of Cyrillic, Greek, Arabic or Hebrew, 24 of Chinese, Japanese or Korean, or 18 emoji. A user writing in Japanese has between 15 and 24 characters to work with.
- **Passwords aren't normalized.** The guideline says "the verifier SHOULD apply the normalization process for stabilized strings using the Normalization Form Canonical Composition (NFC)". Goiabada counts, checks and hashes the characters as typed, so `café` typed with a precomposed `é` and typed with an `e` and a combining accent are two different passwords. Most keyboards type the same form each time, but a password typed on one device may not sign in on another.

## What switching does

Switching policy, to `nist` or any other, holds the next password each user sets to it. Passwords already set aren't checked again: sign-in compares the password with its hash and judges nothing, so nobody is stopped, prompted or flagged for a password an earlier policy accepted. That's what the guideline asks too: it applies its rules when a password is set or changed, and forbids forcing changes on a schedule.

To give a user a password that meets the new policy, open **Admin**, **Users**, the user, and their **Authentication** tab, and use **New password**.

<Aside type="caution">
Switching also holds the Admin API's [create user](/reference/api/admin/operations/createuser/) and [set password](/reference/api/admin/operations/updateuserpassword/) to the new policy. Check what your provisioning scripts send before you switch: a password the new policy refuses is answered `400` with the `error_code` that says why, and no user is created.
</Aside>
